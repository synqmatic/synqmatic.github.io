About this series. This is a live, anonymized case study of running the AI FDE process with a real team. We'll call the company Company X and the team Team T. We're working through the process in order (audit, judgment, build, eval, deploy, observe), starting with one of Team T's primary workflows, the work the team actually needs done. The audit of that workflow shows which smaller base workflows it depends on, and those get their own posts. Details are generalized on purpose, and nothing here names a company, customer or product.

Where we left off

The first eight posts covered BW1, task management, from audit through a first working version tested against a fake model. We are now starting the process again from the top with a primary workflow, PW1: new product reviews. BW1 stays in progress. PW1 will tell us which base workflows come next.

The setup

The audit describes how the work really happens, exceptions included, before anyone decides what to automate. This post covers the audit of PW1, and then applies the drill-down step: each part of PW1 that other workflows also rely on becomes a candidate base workflow, scored on the same six signals as BW1. Anything we have not measured is labeled as an estimate, and anything we don't know is labeled unknown.

What PW1 is

When Company X ships a new product release, Team T walks its customers through what changed. There are two tracks, and they stay separate:

  • Release track. Runs for every release, for every customer. The source material is the release notes plus internal context. It covers the latest release only, not the gap between a customer's current version and the new one. The output is one generic summary document, reused unchanged for all customers.
  • Roadmap track. Runs when a customer asks, or when the topic comes up in a quarterly review. The source material is product roadmap material. The output is always its own document, never merged with a release summary, even when the timing overlaps.

If a customer wants depth on one feature, that request leaves PW1 and becomes a separate product workshop workflow. It is out of scope here.

How it runs today

  1. The trigger arrives (a release notice, or a roadmap question).
  2. The team member gathers the source material for that track.
  3. The team member drafts the summary document.
  4. For each customer, the team member chooses a call or a sent document, and delivers it. The choice is a judgment call each time.

Where it strains

The shareable check is informal. Some roadmap and internal material is not meant for customers. Customer agreements are already in place, so the agreement is not the gap. The gap is that deciding what is safe to include is ad hoc, relies on one person's judgment, and is sometimes missed. We have not checked whether other team members draw the line in the same place. If they don't, that is a policy question to settle before anything is encoded.

The roadmap trigger is invisible. It depends on someone catching it mid-conversation. At this volume, we would keep it human-initiated rather than build detection.

Most of the time is in delivery. Drafting takes about an hour per release (estimate). Delivery takes 45 to 60 minutes per customer (estimate), which is roughly 6 to 8 hours per release across about eight customers. Delivery is a conversation, and it stays with a person.

The neighbours

Upstream is the release notice and the notes themselves. Team T does not control their format, but they are final before the trigger fires, so this side is stable.

Downstream is the customer conversation and any follow-up it creates. Because delivery is most of the time, speeding up drafting will not shorten the end-to-end workflow much. We expect the time to stay in delivery, not disappear. That is a prediction, not an observation.

Baseline

Unmeasured. The figures above are one team member's recollection, so treat them as an order of magnitude. Together they come to roughly 3 to 4 hours a month for a set of about eight customers (estimate). Of that, drafting is about 2 hours a quarter at most (about an hour per release, plus about an hour when the roadmap track runs). Only the drafting is something a system could take over, so the recoverable time is small. Team size is unknown. The value case rests more on consistency and on closing the shareable-check gap than on hours. A measured baseline should replace these estimates before anything is built.

What we don't know yet

  • Team size, and whether other team members do this work the same way.
  • A measured baseline for any step.
  • Where the line between shareable and internal-only sits, and whether it is consistent across the team.
  • Whether the source material is covered by the approved AI tool's data rules.
  • How many other workflows really share the drafting step.
