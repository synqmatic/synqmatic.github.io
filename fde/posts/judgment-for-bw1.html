About this series. This is a live, anonymized case study of running the AI FDE process with a real team. We'll call the company Company X and the team Team T. We're working through the process in order (audit, judgment, build, eval, deploy, observe), starting with the small base workflows that Team T's main workflows are built from. Details are generalized on purpose, and nothing here names a company, customer or product.

Where we left off

The audit of Base Workflow 1 (BW1, task management) landed on a provisional score of 25 out of 30 and a green risk tag. It is recurring, language-heavy, pattern-based work on internal data. One number came out of it: a team member's rough estimate of 2 to 3 hours a week spent managing tasks. That is a recollection, not a measurement, and it swings with how much detail each task carries.

The judgment stage

The judgment stage answers a different question: not whether AI can help here, but where, step by step. It runs three gates in order, and each one can end the assessment.

Gate 1: does this workflow need changing at all?

The default answer is no. We decline if any of these tests fails.

TestResult for BW1
VolumePasses. It runs continuously across every customer, though the exact volume is unmeasured
ROIPasses on the working estimate. About 2 to 3 hours a week per member, roughly 100 to 150 hours a year (an estimate), is the ceiling, and only part of it is work a model could touch
Already solvedPasses. There is no standard process and no automation today
SensitivityPasses. Green tag, internal process
StabilityPasses, as far as we know. Nothing suggests the process is about to change

BW1 clears Gate 1. The ROI test rests on an unmeasured estimate, so it should be re-checked once a baseline is measured.

Gate 2: can plain code do it?

This gate comes before reaching for a model. Code is cheaper, testable and auditable, and it does not hallucinate. Using a model where code would do is the most common way effort gets wasted in this field.

Most of BW1 lands here. Looking up which member owns a customer, checking that a task has its required fields, and writing the task into the tool are all rules and lookups.

Gate 3: which steps need judgment?

Only steps that are irreducibly non-deterministic get a model: fuzzy classification, extraction from unstructured text, summarization. In BW1 that comes to two:

  • Drafting the task from an email, a chat thread or a call transcript: pulling out what needs doing and the relevant details.
  • Matching a task to a category, where definitions are clear but real tasks often fit more than one.

Step allocation

There are four categories, not three. Human approves (a machine did the step and a person gates it) and human performs (the person does the step) are different things.

Sanity checks on the finished sheet. Two rows are model steps, which is the expected shape: one or two model calls, a few deterministic steps and one approval point. If a model had landed on most rows, the map would be wrong. There is a human-approves row, so the workflow is safe to build.

Coverage is not a step. A task always belongs to its owner. While the owner is away, the manager follows up on it, which is a person doing the work, not a routing rule.

What this tells us

The category decision is the heart of it. It is the one step where a model's judgment really matters, and it is also the step the audit flagged. We have not yet checked whether team members resolve category overlaps the same way. If they do not, there is no rule to teach a model yet. That is a policy question, and it has to be settled first.

Most of the value is not AI. Seven of nine steps are deterministic or stay with a person. The model's job is narrow.

What we do not know yet

  • Category consistency across team members is unchecked.
  • A measured baseline is needed to replace the working estimate before anything is built.
