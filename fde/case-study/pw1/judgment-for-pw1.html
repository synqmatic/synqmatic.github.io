About this series. This is a live, anonymized case study of running the AI FDE process with a real team. We'll call the company Company X and the team Team T. We're working through the process in order (audit, judgment, build, eval, deploy, observe), starting with one of Team T's primary workflows, the work the team actually needs done. The audit of that workflow shows which smaller base workflows it depends on, and those get their own posts. Details are generalized on purpose, and nothing here names a company, customer or product.

Where we left off

The audit of Primary Workflow 1 (PW1, new product reviews) landed on a provisional score of 26 out of 30 and a yellow risk tag. It has two tracks. The release track runs for every release and produces one generic document for all customers. The roadmap track runs on request and produces its own document. Three findings came out of it: the check for what is safe to show customers is informal today, most of the time goes into delivering to customers rather than drafting, and nothing has been measured yet. The hour figures are one team member's estimates.

The judgment stage asks a different question: not can AI help here, but where, step by step. It runs three gates in order, and each one can end the assessment.

Gate 1: does this workflow need changing at all?

The default answer is no. We decline if any of these hold.

TestResult for PW1
VolumePasses. It recurs with every release, for about eight customers (estimate), plus the occasional roadmap request
ROIBorderline. The whole workflow is roughly 3 to 4 hours a month (estimate), and only drafting, about 2 hours a quarter at most, is work a system could take over. The case rests on consistency and on closing the shareable-check gap, not on hours
Already solvedPasses. It is fully manual today
SensitivityPasses. Sensitivity sits in one step, deciding what is safe to show customers, not in every step
StabilityPasses, as far as we know. Nothing suggests the process is about to change

PW1 clears Gate 1, narrowly. The ROI test rests on unmeasured estimates, so it should be re-checked once a baseline is measured. If the measured drafting time is lower than we think, this workflow may not repay a build.

Gate 2: can plain code do it?

This gate comes before reaching for a model. Code is cheaper, testable, auditable and can't hallucinate. Using a model where code would do is the most common form of wasting effort in this field.

Most of PW1 lands here. Reacting to a release notice, pulling the release notes and internal context, reusing one document for every customer and sending a document once it is approved are all lookups and rules.

Gate 3: which steps need judgment?

Only steps that are irreducibly non-deterministic get a model: fuzzy classification, extraction from unstructured text, summarization. In PW1 that comes to two:

  • Labeling source items as shareable with customers or internal only. The boundary is fuzzy and the material is prose.
  • Drafting the summary document from the items labeled shareable.

Step allocation

There are four categories, not three. Human approves (a machine did the step and a person gates it) and human performs (the person does the step) are different things.

Sanity checks on the finished sheet. Two rows are model steps, which is the expected shape: one or two model calls, a few deterministic steps and one approval point. There is a human-approves row, so it is safe to build.

Boundaries. The model may label items, draft the document and flag anything unclear. It may not include an item labeled internal only in any document a customer sees, treat its own label as final, or deliver anything to a customer.

What this tells us

The label is the heart of it. It is the one step where a model's judgment matters, and the audit flagged it. We have not checked whether team members draw the line in the same place. If they don't, there is no rule to teach a model yet. That is a policy question, and it has to be settled first.

Most of the value is not AI, and most of the time stays with a person. Five of seven steps are deterministic or stay with a person, and the biggest block of time, delivery in step 7, is a person's step by design. We expect the end-to-end time to move less than the drafting time does. That is a prediction, not an observation.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether the source material already marks what is customer-facing. If it does, step 4 could become a deterministic check and the workflow would need only one model call.
  • Where the line between shareable and internal only sits, and whether it is consistent across the team.
  • Whether the source material is covered by the approved AI tool's data rules.
  • A measured baseline, which decides whether the ROI test still passes.
