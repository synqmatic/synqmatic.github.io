Synqmatic / AI in the software lifecycle / Where AI sits in the software lifecycle

AI touches every stage of the software lifecycle, but the constraint moves to review and verification.

The short version

  • What I tried: reading the Anthropic, DORA and vendor material on the AI-native lifecycle, and mapping what AI does at each of the six standard stages.
  • What happened: code generation got fastest, so planning, review, testing and governance became the slow parts. In the 2025 DORA survey of about 5,000 people, roughly 90% used AI at work, yet about 30% had little or no trust in the code it produced.
  • What I would do next: pick one stage, probably verification, and measure it on a real change with a dated baseline.

The problem and the context

Most teams still run the same six stages: plan, design, build, test, deploy, maintain. AI is usually added at the build stage and the rest of the process is left alone. Before running any experiments of my own, I wanted a plain map of what AI changes at each stage and what stays with a person.

This is desk research. I built and measured nothing, and most of the numbers below come from surveys and vendor reports, so treat them as signals rather than proof.

Where AI sits in the lifecycle

Stage What AI does What stays with a person
Plan Turns emails, tickets and notes into draft requirements and flags ambiguities. The specification becomes the main thing a person writes. Trade-off decisions. AI has no business context.
Design Proposes an initial architecture and prototypes. Design turns into constraints the agents must respect. Reviewing and revising the proposal, and domain-specific choices.
Build Writes code, tests and documentation. Pairing an engineer with an agent is now the usual mode. Architecture, edge cases, and reading what comes back.
Test Generates unit, property-based and visual regression tests, and runs continuous evals during implementation. Integration scenarios, and checking that tests verify the requirements and not just the code's own assumptions.
Deploy Runs layered agent reviews and policy checks in the pipeline, with hooks acting as approval gates. Regulated and critical changes. In practice, deployment autonomy is lower than teams plan for.
Maintain Watches live systems, diagnoses breached controls and feeds findings back into the next plan. Incident response, and owning monitoring of model behaviour after release.

The common thread is that the lifecycle stops being a line and becomes a loop. Each stage commits an artifact, such as a spec or a plan, that the next stage reads, and the commit history doubles as the audit trail.

What the numbers say

  • Faster generation is not faster delivery. LinearB's 2026 data covers more than 8.1 million pull requests across 4,800 teams. At the 75th percentile, AI-assisted PRs were 2.6 times larger than unassisted ones (408 against 157 lines). For the median team, feature-branch throughput rose 15% while main-branch throughput fell 7%.
  • Felt speed and real speed differ. In a controlled study reported by ToTheNew, developers took about 19% longer with AI allowed, yet still estimated it had made them about 20% faster.
  • Trust and checking do not match. A Sonar 2026 survey, reported secondhand, found 96% of developers do not fully trust AI-generated code, while only 48% always check it before committing.
  • AI amplifies what is already there. DORA's headline finding is that AI magnifies the strengths of high-performing teams and the weaknesses of struggling ones.

What the evidence does not show

  • Almost every source is a summary of a summary. I did not reach the original data for the Sonar and controlled-study figures.
  • Consultancies report large gains, such as requirements work being 40% faster. Those are self-reported by firms that sell AI services, so I left them out of the numbers above.
  • A widely quoted figure says AI-agent PRs take 5.3 times longer to be picked up. That measures waiting time before a reviewer starts, not how long the review takes.

What I would change

I started with the picture of AI as a speed-up for coding inside an unchanged lifecycle. After reading, I would design the verification stage first and treat generation as the cheap part. Sources also draw a line between AI-augmented lifecycles, where the stages and handoffs stay the same, and AI-native ones, where the stages blur. Most teams appear to be in the first group while adopting tools built for the second.

Open questions

  • On a real team, where does the time go: waiting for a reviewer, active review, or integration testing?
  • Do AI-written tests catch defects that the AI-written code hides, or do they share its blind spots?
  • Which stage gains the most from writing the specification first?
